CHINA EQUITIES

Renminbi Appreciation, Industrial Upgrading and Equity Repricing

HENGFENG CAPITAL · RESEARCH PERSPECTIVE—9 November 2026

When looking for an equity-market turning point, investors often start with consumption, orders and corporate earnings. Share prices, however, frequently move ahead of fundamentals. While profits have yet to improve materially, changes in exchange rates, capital flows and the supply of and demand for assets can already begin to reprice the market.

Key Takeaway

We believe conditions are developing for a period of equity valuation recovery. The initial drivers may be a shift in how companies convert foreign-currency receipts as the renminbi strengthens, and a repricing of equity risk as pressure from new issuance eases. Earnings improvement may come later.

Renminbi appreciation also deserves a longer-term look through the lens of industrial structure. As Chinese manufacturers compete increasingly on technology, efficiency and supply-chain capabilities, a stronger currency need not erode export competitiveness across the board. It may reinforce industrial upgrading. The exchange rate can therefore matter on two horizons. Over time, it may alter how China’s export industries compete. In the near term, it may change when companies convert foreign-currency revenues and how domestic investors allocate capital.

Does a stronger renminbi weaken China’s export competitiveness?

A common concern is that appreciation makes Chinese products more expensive overseas and directly weakens their competitiveness. The economics of low-cost manufacturing are an incomplete guide to the relationship between China’s exports and its currency today. Historically, China’s exporters drew an important advantage from labour and production costs and scale. Appreciation can still squeeze margins and weaken the position of exporters that compete mainly on price and have little margin to absorb it.

The basis of competition has changed. In new energy vehicles, power batteries, photovoltaic equipment, some electronic components and industrial equipment, Chinese manufacturers have built supply chains spanning raw materials, components and final assembly. These industries compete on accumulated technology, production efficiency, scale, supply-chain depth and product quality as well as cost.

Consider a Chinese industrial component that sells for US$100 against a competing product at US$150. If the USD/CNY rate falls 5%, holding other factors constant, the Chinese supplier would need to raise its dollar price by about 5.3% to preserve the same renminbi revenue. Its product would still be substantially cheaper. Higher productivity, more consistent quality and shorter lead times would further reduce the likelihood that customers switch suppliers.

For firms with technological barriers to entry, supply-chain advantages and strong market positions, moderate appreciation may show up mainly in near-term margins, with less effect on order volumes. It can also lower the renminbi cost of imported equipment, critical inputs and some technology investments. The impact on exports is therefore likely to vary across industries.

Moderate appreciation could accelerate industrial upgrading

Renminbi appreciation and industrial upgrading may reinforce each other. As manufacturers move into higher-value activities, stronger technology, efficiency, quality and supplier networks allow some products to withstand a stronger currency.

Appreciation puts greater pressure on low-margin exporters. The same dollar revenue converts into fewer renminbi. Companies that cannot raise prices, improve productivity or cut costs may come under considerable operating pressure. Some firms may accelerate investment in production technology and higher-value products. Some labour-intensive steps may shift to lower-cost locations. This could direct more domestic resources towards technology-intensive and higher-value industries. Moderate appreciation may help drive changes in industrial structure.

Industrial upgrading can in turn support the renminbi. When export strength rests increasingly on technology, quality and efficient supply chains, the economy may rely less on currency depreciation to maintain price competitiveness. Stronger industries can withstand more appreciation, and moderate appreciation can encourage further productivity and product improvements.

Labour-intensive manufacturing continues to matter for employment, supply-chain support and export income. Appreciation that comes too quickly can also weaken cash flow and leave firms with less room to invest. We see more scope for adjustment when the exchange rate strengthens gradually and in an orderly way. Appreciation may reflect a shift in Chinese manufacturing from competing mainly on price to competing on a broader set of industrial capabilities. We will watch high-value products’ share of global markets, exporters’ earnings quality and their ability to retain an advantage with a stronger renminbi, alongside headline export growth.

Appreciation is changing companies’ currency conversion decisions

Appreciation may affect domestic cash flows more immediately through corporate foreign-exchange conversion. A question we have followed closely is when foreign-currency revenue from China’s exports will begin to circulate as renminbi at home. Export growth does not automatically improve domestic liquidity. An exporter can convert dollar receipts or hold dollar assets to earn interest or fund overseas purchases. The decision depends on exchange-rate and interest-rate expectations and anticipated operating needs as well as export volumes.

Some firms may retain dollars when they expect the renminbi to weaken. A sustained move in the opposite direction changes the calculation. At USD/CNY 7.00, US$10 million converts into RMB 70 million. At 6.80 it yields RMB 68 million. For businesses with renminbi wages, input costs, debt or domestic investments, the opportunity cost of retaining dollars rises. Appreciation may bring forward conversion of foreign-currency receipts that firms had previously retained. A feedback loop can develop as earlier conversions add to dollar supply and renminbi demand, which in turn can support the exchange rate.

State Administration of Foreign Exchange (SAFE) data show that in August 2026, foreign currency sold to banks totalled US$251.8 billion, while banks sold US$203.3 billion in foreign currency, leaving a surplus of US$48.5 billion. In September the corresponding figures were US$294.2 billion and US$238.0 billion. The surplus rose to US$56.2 billion, up about 15.9% from August. SAFE also said the share of foreign-currency receipts converted by companies and other market participants increased in September. The share of payments funded by buying foreign currency also rose, and the banking figures include banks’ own transactions. The full increase in the surplus therefore cannot be attributed to exporters converting their receipts.

We think appreciation is already affecting how some companies hold foreign exchange, and that previously accumulated receipts may be entering a new conversion window. If the trend continues, it could change the composition of corporate cash holdings and support renminbi cash flows within China.

Equities may not fully reflect the effect of currency conversion

Companies can use renminbi proceeds from currency conversion to pay wages, service debt, fund operations, pay dividends or expand investment. They may also hold the proceeds as deposits. Even so, the shift matters at a macro level. When firms convert more foreign-currency receipts, the amount of renminbi they hold and how they use it may change. As funds circulate through businesses, households, financial institutions and asset managers, they could improve domestic funding conditions and generate demand for assets. Conversion changes the currency and ownership structure of funds. Its effect on renminbi liquidity in the banking system depends on counterparties, banks’ positions and whether the central bank participates. What matters for markets is how companies and investors deploy the proceeds.

Where equities have already corrected and risk premia remain relatively high, a small change in allocations can have a larger price effect. Weak risk appetite can depress valuations even with stable earnings, as investors demand more compensation for bearing risk. When funding conditions improve and investors are more willing to take risk, valuations can rise before earnings recover.

The question is whether greater currency conversion leads to a reallocation of domestic funds that helps compress equity risk premia. More corporate conversion, low domestic funding costs, stronger equity-fund subscriptions and declining risk premia would together make a liquidity-led recovery more plausible. That is how valuations could improve ahead of earnings.

Slower IPO activity is changing the balance of equity supply and demand

The supply side is also changing. National Business Daily, citing Wind data, reported that A-share IPO proceeds fell from RMB 26.053 billion in September 2026 to RMB 10.983 billion in October, down about 57.8%. New listings fell from 21 to nine. No new IPO applications were accepted in October and only four applicants went before an initial review committee.

When fresh capital is limited, investors must absorb new listings, follow-on issuance and large shareholder sales alongside their existing holdings. With less new equity to absorb, the same pool of investment funds can provide more support to shares already in the market. IPO proceeds eventually fund business activity and remain within the financial system. During subscriptions, new issuance still uses part of investors’ available allocation. The pace of primary-market financing can therefore affect the marginal balance of supply and demand in the secondary market.

We are watching whether financing activity is shifting towards greater emphasis on the quality of issuance, investors’ capacity to absorb it and investment returns. A persistent shift could ease pressure on existing equities. One month of lower IPO proceeds is too little to establish a longer-term trend. IPO registrations and issuance processes continued in November. A longer run of financing data, together with follow-on offerings and shareholder sales, will give a clearer picture.

Hong Kong’s quieter IPO market may reflect a broader shift in financing

Hong Kong has also seen a slowdown. National Business Daily reported that six companies filed listing applications with HKEX in October 2026, down from 15 in September. Hong Kong’s Securities and Futures Commission has continued to strengthen oversight of listing application quality, sponsor due diligence and related conduct.

The mainland and Hong Kong have different regulatory systems and investor bases, but their recent financing trends can be assessed through a common lens. A healthy capital market needs both to raise funds for companies and to offer returns to investors. If issuance grows faster than the capital and risk capacity available to absorb it, valuations can come under pressure. A slower pace of issuance and higher listing standards could improve the balance of supply and demand and direct more capital towards stronger companies.

Hong Kong still requires a separate assessment. Fewer applications do not necessarily lead to persistently lower proceeds. A slowdown may reflect review and scheduling decisions or weaker investor demand. Renminbi proceeds from currency conversion only affect Hong Kong equity demand through channels such as southbound flows, cross-border investment and international portfolio allocation.

We are watching whether equity supply is growing more slowly in both markets and whether investors are placing more weight on asset quality and returns. If confirmed, that would put more emphasis on the earnings quality and investment value of existing companies in market pricing.

Exchange-rate management makes the conversion window conditional

On 3 November, the People’s Bank of China announced a two-percentage-point increase in the foreign-currency deposit reserve requirement for financial institutions, from 4% to 6%, effective 15 November. The move came during a period of renminbi appreciation. A higher requirement reduces the foreign-currency liquidity banks can deploy and can affect foreign-exchange supply, demand and expectations. We read it as an attempt to manage the pace of appreciation. Whether the trend has ended remains an open question.

As appreciation expectations build, exporters may gradually convert a larger share of receipts. A faster move may bring some conversions forward to protect renminbi proceeds. A move that is too fast can squeeze margins, weaken business confidence and raise the risk of a short-term reversal.

Our preferred scenario is gradual renminbi appreciation in a relatively stable environment, rising corporate willingness to convert foreign-currency receipts and continued export competitiveness based on technology, efficiency and supply chains. Gradual adjustment may support steadier funding expectations and a more stable environment for industrial upgrading than a sudden wave of conversions. If appreciation expectations reverse, the incentive to convert may weaken. We will track the direction and pace of the currency, actual corporate conversions and exporter profitability.

Why valuations could recover ahead of earnings

Potential changes in funding and the starting level of valuations may reinforce each other. Some of the earlier decline in equities reflected weaker risk appetite and higher risk premia, as well as concerns about earnings. Even lower risk-free rates may give little support if investors demand substantially more compensation for equity risk. If funding conditions improve and investors reassess the balance of risk and return, valuations can recover before fundamentals do. Turning points in the economy and in equity markets can arrive at different times.

Markets can price in anticipated policy effects, funding conditions and earnings. A stronger renminbi may change conversion behaviour and support domestic cash flows, slower IPO financing may reduce pressure from new supply, and some equities have already seen substantial valuation compression. If those forces coincide, we see scope for a period of valuation recovery over the next one to two months. The initial drivers would likely be expectations for liquidity and a lower risk premium. The effect on company earnings would take longer.

A stronger renminbi could also sharpen the distinction between exporters. We are watching businesses with technological advantages, established supply-chain positions and resilient profitability whose valuations fell as risk appetite weakened. More favourable liquidity is unlikely to sustain a recovery in companies with deteriorating fundamentals and limited competitive barriers.

Four developments to watch over the next one to two months

First, whether appreciation continues to encourage corporate conversion. Watch the exchange rate, corporate conversion ratios, banks’ net foreign-exchange conversion on behalf of clients and corporate foreign-currency deposits. A relatively stable exchange rate alongside a higher willingness to convert would support the case for stronger domestic cash flows.

Second, whether conversion proceeds reach equity allocations. Watch equity ETF flows, equity-fund subscriptions, execution of company buybacks and equity risk premia. A currency exchange by itself does not increase equity exposure. Low funding costs, stronger equity buying and falling risk premia together would offer more evidence of a funding-led valuation recovery.

Third, whether new equity supply remains subdued. Track actual A-share IPO proceeds, follow-on financing, major shareholder sales, Hong Kong IPO financing and southbound flows. If new financing remains moderate as equity allocations improve, the supply-demand balance may become more favourable to existing shares.

Fourth, whether Chinese exporters maintain their competitive position as the renminbi strengthens. Track export growth and global market shares for higher-value products, exporter margins and the cost of imported equipment and key inputs. Continued competitiveness alongside improvements in product mix and earnings quality would support the view that appreciation and upgrading can reinforce each other. It would also help identify which industries can support equity earnings over the longer term.

Equities may be entering a new window for valuation recovery

Taken together, developments in the exchange rate, corporate conversion, industrial competitiveness and equity issuance suggest that conditions for a period of valuation recovery are building. Over the long term, Chinese manufacturers increasingly compete through technology, efficiency and supply chains. Moderate appreciation can encourage further adjustment, with effects that vary by industry.

Over the medium term, appreciation is changing how some companies hold and convert foreign exchange. Previously accumulated receipts may start to circulate as renminbi domestically. In the near term, if domestic capital allocations shift while pressure from new issuance eases, equity risk premia could fall and valuations recover ahead of a material improvement in corporate earnings.

Our base case is that moderate renminbi appreciation and stronger manufacturing competitiveness could reinforce each other, while changes in corporate currency conversion and easing equity supply could help catalyse the next phase of valuation recovery. The main risks are an appreciation fast enough to hurt exporter margins, a failure of conversion proceeds to reach equity allocations, renewed issuance pressure and further deterioration in earnings expectations. The changes observed so far warrant close attention.

Industrial competitiveness shapes long-term value. Funding conditions and risk appetite shape near-term prices. Renminbi appreciation could affect both China’s export structure and domestic asset allocations. A shift in funding and equity supply could drive the initial repricing, while earnings would determine how far the recovery can go.
